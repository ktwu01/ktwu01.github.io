Five YC rebels: why were they selected, why were they expelled, and why did they rise again?

> Author: [Koutian Wu](https://www.linkedin.com/in/ktwu01/); [GitHub: ktwu01](https://github.com/ktwu01/)

## Introduction

Y Combinator is Silicon Valley's most prestigious startup accelerator, founded by Paul Graham and a few partners in 2005. In his essay "What We Look for in Founders," Paul Graham praised the naughty streak in founders: that piratical gleam, the delight in breaking rules that don't matter. He wrote:

> "They're not Goody Two-Shoes type good. Morally, they care about getting the big questions right, but not about observing proprieties... They delight in breaking rules, but not rules that matter."

![Paul Graham Blog](/images/yc-rebels/PaulGrahamBlog.png)

Paul Graham believed the best founders tend to have a rebellious streak: they challenge the status quo, question authority, and carry a slightly dangerous air. YC's selection mechanism is, at its core, a search for this kind of controlled madness.

But there is an interesting paradox here. The very traits YC prizes most — independent thinking, challenging authority, radical transparency — became grounds for expulsion once turned against YC itself.

The stories of Breck Yunits, Paul Biggar, Katia Damer, Andrew Torba, and Parker Conrad are not five versions of one story. They reveal several different boundaries: confidentiality, public criticism, gender issues, regulatory misconduct, and harassment. The truly interesting contradiction is that an organization whose mission is to select independent thinkers must still enforce boundaries on independent thinkers.

To understand what expulsion means, you first have to understand the territory from which one is banished. Y Combinator is not just a source of funding; it is a platform for social legitimacy and operating leverage. Its mechanisms rest on three pillars: Bookface, Hacker News, and the alumni network.

## YC's Three Mechanisms

### Bookface: The Community Commons

At the core of the YC experience is Bookface. Technically, it is a proprietary internal platform combining a forum, a wiki, and LinkedIn-like directory features. Culturally, it is the public square of the YC community.

Bookface access is effectively lifelong, like a digital green card for YC founders. It gives them a protective moat, letting them solve problems faster than non-YC peers. Revoking Bookface access is the primary administrative measure taken against removed founders — like revoking a citizen's passport in the digital age: you still exist, but your identity is gone.

### Hacker News

Bookface is private; Hacker News (HN) is public. HN is owned and operated by YC, and its moderation is handled by YC staff (the most famous moderator, dang, is Dan Gackle). Although HN presents itself as an open aggregator for computer science and startups, critics, including removed founder Breck Yunits, allege that it functions as a subtle narrative-control tool.

HN's ranking algorithm shapes what hundreds of thousands of technologists see each day. Critics note that removing or flagging a post can make a story vanish from the front page, though YC has never admitted using this to suppress narratives unfavorable to itself. For YC founders, HN is a crucial launchpad. For YC dissidents, HN often becomes a hostile environment where their criticism gets buried or dismissed as noise. In the case studies below, we will repeatedly see the interplay between internal exclusion and public invisibility.

### The Alumni Network

The most effective enforcement mechanism is social. YC alumni status is a powerful group badge. When a founder is marked as unwelcome, the network effect reverses. Instead of opening doors, a founder's name may trigger private warnings. This soft ban is often more damaging than revoking login credentials: being removed from private WhatsApp groups, given the cold shoulder on Demo Day, and shunned by alumni wary of association.

## Five Removed Founders

### 1. Breck Yunits: The Expelled Whistleblower

Yunits is a classic hacker: obsessed with truth, data visualization, and better systems (Scrolling/Tree Notation). YC appreciated his unbounded geek spirit and ambition to rebuild the world. He was the kind of person who wanted to change not just products, but the rules of the game.

The problem came when that rebuilding impulse pointed at YC's own business model. YC appreciated his idealism about optimizing the world through technology and logic, but when he tried to optimize YC's profit-distribution mechanism with his "Carry" proposal, the idealism became a threat.

Yunits' Carry proposal argued for creating a new fund or accelerator program where founders would share carried interest among themselves. He believed such a program would be more attractive to startups and mentors because it better aligned participants' incentives. This directly challenged the core economics of YC's venture model (charging funds a 2% management fee plus 20% carried interest); simply put, he wanted to re-cut the cake.

Beyond the Carry proposal, Yunits also publicly criticized Hacker News' moderation. He could not tolerate covert manipulation in a community that claimed to be open.

Tensions eventually escalated into a complete break. Yunits increasingly criticized what he saw as censorship on Hacker News, alleging that the platform manipulated post visibility to favor views YC supported and suppress dissent. In a Reddit discussion about YC's internal culture, Yunits (posting as breck) wrote scathingly:

> "I don't give a shit about being kicked out of Dickface. What really pisses me off is the endless censorship on HackerNews and their daily censorship. I'm not asking them to upvote my posts, I'm asking them to allow others to downvote my posts. The fact that they're so afraid of people seeing what I write exposes just how cowardly they are."

![YC-kickoff-breck](/images/yc-rebels/YC-kickoff-breck.png)

This was a genuine collision: Yunits did not merely break rules; he attacked the institution's courage and integrity.

YC's response was comprehensive: according to Yunits, he was kicked off Bookface, blocked by the official @ycombinator Twitter account, and even his mail server was completely cut off. As he put it: "They blocked all @ycombinator.com email addresses outright, preventing me from sending mail."

This level of lockdown suggests Yunits was classified as hostile, not merely a misbehaving alumnus. For an institution, an uncontrollable internal critic, even a well-intentioned one, is more dangerous than an external competitor.

### 2. Paul Biggar: The Vaccine-Line Leaker

Biggar, founder of CircleCI and Darklang, has always been an advocate of transparency culture. YC encourages founders to be honest and direct, skip the PR speak. Early on, that candor is a cornerstone of trust.

The problem came when that candor pointed inward. YC appreciated his obsession with facts and his iconoclastic geek spirit, but when he made the community's privileged behavior (cutting the vaccine line) public, candor became betrayal of the tribe.

In March 2021, two YC founders posted on Bookface about how they bypassed vaccine eligibility requirements, offering other founders advice on cutting the line. Their tactics included misrepresenting their situations and exploiting loopholes in county booking websites. Biggar considered this serious moral failure and tech-elite privilege, and tweeted about it publicly. Notably, per TechCrunch's reporting at the time, he did not publish screenshots or identifying details; YC stated he was removed for violating its prohibition on externally sharing internal information. The two sides defined "leaking" in opposite ways: to Biggar, he exposed unethical conduct; to YC, he broke confidentiality rules.

For YC, this was not just a leak but a flagrant violation of the community's culture of confidentiality. Bookface's first rule: what happens on Bookface stays on Bookface.

![Paul Biggar Tweet](/images/yc-rebels/paul_biggar_tweet.png)

Biggar proved willing to betray the "fraternity" of YC for moral principle. In some sense that is the highest character; in a closed fraternity network like YC, it is the most unforgivable crime.

### 3. Ekaterina "Katia" Damer: The Gender Battle

YC likes to call itself meritocratic, supportive of unpopular-but-correct views. Dr. Damer, CEO of Prolific, seemed exactly such a founder: a woman willing to speak up.

But when the status quo she challenged was Silicon Valley's deeply entrenched male-dominated culture, she found the limits of YC's inclusiveness. YC admired her tenacity as a female scientist and her steadfast conviction, but that conviction made her unable to tolerate misogyny within the community, leading her to criticize not just one individual (the author of Chaos Monkeys) but the community's indulgence of him — crossing YC's tolerance boundary.

Damer's conflict with the YC network was rooted in Silicon Valley's gender dynamics. In May 2021, Apple hired Antonio García Martínez (YC alumnus, AdGrok founder). Almost immediately, Apple employees launched a petition to fire him over passages in his memoir they considered misogynistic.

In Chaos Monkeys, García Martínez described women in the San Francisco Bay Area as "soft, weak, cosseted, naive," contrasting them with the "lush, fertile" women he admired elsewhere. The passage triggered the 2021 Apple employee petition, and Apple fired him within days.

Inside YC, the reaction to García Martínez was largely protective. Some prominent alumni and partners framed his firing as a victory for cancel culture and a loss for free speech, and the alumni network rallied behind him.

Damer refused to compromise. She posted on Bookface asking whether YC was an inclusive organization, arguing that continuing down this path would cost YC its good reputation, and criticizing the network's defense of García Martínez. She believed defending such views created a hostile environment for female founders within the YC network.

YC co-founder Jessica Livingston replied: "YC is not reflecting." Her reply received hundreds of likes; Damer's post got only a handful. Furious, Damer posted screenshots of the exchange to LinkedIn and Twitter and withdrew all her previous praise of YC founders.

Two weeks later, Damer was removed from the YC alumni network. Asked whether she regretted speaking out publicly, she told TechCrunch:

> "People use rules when they don't know what else to do. This is about courage, this is about actually standing up for what is right."

![YC-kickoff-damer](/images/yc-rebels/YC-kickoff-Damer.png)

Damer's removal highlights a critical boundary in YC culture. Though the accelerator prides itself on investing in female founders, its internal culture remains male-dominated and libertarian.

YC tolerates male alternative humor and behavior (often packaged as high agency), but policing that behavior is treated as cultural conflict or harmful conduct. Damer's removal showed YC placing the comfort of the majority (men) above the safety and inclusion of the minority (women).

### 4. Parker Conrad: The Growth Zealot Felled by Compliance

Conrad is the walking embodiment of "Move Fast and Break Things." From Zenefits to Rippling, he has shown extraordinary execution and growth-at-all-costs ambition — precisely the spirit Paul Graham celebrates.

He founded Zenefits (YC W13), an HR software company whose growth outpaced any SaaS company in history, reaching a $4.5 billion valuation within two years. Rocket speed.

But that hypergrowth came at a cost. In 2016, Zenefits was revealed to have built a software tool called "The Macro," which let insurance brokers evade mandatory state licensing requirements by keeping training modules running while they slept. The regulatory scandal forced Conrad to resign in disgrace. He came under fire from the media, the SEC, and his own board (Andreessen Horowitz).

YC had once appreciated his relentless growth drive and reality-distortion field (a term originally coined for Jobs, describing the charisma that makes teams believe and achieve the seemingly impossible), which returned enormous value to YC. But when that drive spun out of control into a compliance scandal, Conrad went from star founder to toxic asset.

Unlike Biggar or Yunits, Conrad was not removed over a tweet but over reputational damage. After his departure, YC distanced itself from the connection. Notably, years later YC publicly brought him back on camera to discuss his entrepreneurial journey — more institutional rehabilitation than permanent exile.

Conrad described feeling isolated from this group as being treated as radioactive — dangerous contamination on contact. YC saw him not merely as a failure but as a contagion vector; his presence in the YC network could invite regulatory scrutiny onto other startups.

According to SEC filings, in October 2017 Conrad personally paid hundreds of thousands of dollars in penalties, as did Zenefits.

![YC-kickoff-Conrad](/images/yc-rebels/YC-kickoff-Conrad.png)

But Conrad did not disappear. He founded Rippling, another HR/IT company.

- **Compound startup**: Conrad openly criticized YC's standard advice to focus on one thing. He argued that for B2B software, you need multiple integrated product lines forming a matrix to build a true compound startup that solves enterprises' deep needs, rather than a single-point tool.
- **Success**: Rippling is now valued at over $13 billion — more than Zenefits ever was.
- **Founder Mode vindicated**: In 2024, Paul Graham published "Founder Mode," partly inspired by Brian Chesky, which resonated deeply with Conrad's hands-on management style and rejection of professional-manager orthodoxy. Conrad's success forced YC to revisit its own playbook, and Conrad himself went from exile to rehabilitated case study.

### 5. Andrew Torba: The Political Exile

Torba was part of YC W15 (AutomateAds) and represents the absolute outer edge of YC's tolerance. In 2016, he became a vocal Trump supporter. Later, he founded Gab, a free-speech-branded social network that became a haven for the alt-right and white nationalists.

YC had once appreciated his courage to stand against the crowd and his vision of building an independent ecosystem. But when standing apart hardened into concrete political hatred and online harassment, YC drew a line.

Torba was removed from the alumni network in 2016. According to Hacker News discussion records, during a Facebook thread involving YC partner Garry Tan, Torba got into heated arguments with other founders. Among his explosive remarks:

> "All of you: fuck off. Take your morally superior, elitist, virtue signaling bullshit and shove it. I call it like I see it, and I helped meme a President into office, cucks."

![YC-fxxk-off-Torba](/images/yc-rebels/YC-fxxk-off-Torba.png)

Sam Altman issued a statement clarifying that Torba was removed not for his politics (supporting Trump) but for violating harassment policy. Altman wrote: "Unless you think trolling is a political stance worth protecting... he was removed for being a malicious troll."

This case defines the boundary of the "YC punishes rebels" narrative. Dissent (Damer, Biggar), disclosure, regulatory misconduct (Conrad), and harassment (Torba) are not the same thing: the former are byproducts of independent thinking, the latter a red line any institution has good reason to draw. Torba himself argued that for conservatives, expressing belief gets defined by the left as harassment, making the distinction meaningless. But whichever account you accept, lumping his case together with whistleblowers and public critics blurs the whole argument.

## Analysis: Sociology of the Shadow Alumni

These founders share one trait: extreme autonomy. Strong-willed, opinionated, uncompromising — precisely the qualities YC claims to look for in applicants. Ironically, when those qualities are aimed at YC, YC's defenses strike back hard.

Each story carries its own version of the paradox:

- **Breck Yunits**: drawn in for independent thinking, expelled for whistleblowing
- **Paul Biggar**: drawn in for radical transparency, removed for disclosing internal information
- **Katia Damer**: drawn in for challenging the status quo, expelled for challenging sexism
- **Parker Conrad**: drawn in for moving fast, forced out by a regulatory scandal
- **Andrew Torba**: drawn in for free speech, removed for harassment — his case is the counterexample that bounds the narrative

Yet for most of them, removal was not a death sentence:

- Conrad built a bigger company (Rippling)
- Biggar became a moral voice in the industry (Darklang)
- Damer built a market leader (Prolific)
- Yunits kept his intellectual purity (tree notation)
- Torba built Gab — but Gab devolved into a haven for the alt-right, turning him from example into counterexample

This shows YC's network effect, while powerful, is not indispensable. For the most ambitious founders, Bookface's closed circle can even become a burden: it lets you hear only insiders' voices and miss the outside world's opportunities. Being kicked out forces founders to build without the scaffolding of connections and peer validation.

### The Rise of the "Anti-Establishment"

We are witnessing the fragmentation of Silicon Valley consensus. Shadow alumni are forming their own spheres of influence, roughly three camps:

- **Moralists**: Biggar and Damer — after leaving, focused on human rights and fair-labor issues, defining tech's moral boundaries through action
- **Independents**: Conrad and Torba — chose to bypass Silicon Valley's mainstream ecosystem, building their own platforms and stacks without traditional gatekeepers
- **Idealists**: Yunits — insists truth-seeking takes priority over commercial profit

For the sharp observer, these three camps represent different possible futures for the industry beyond an increasingly homogeneous YC/Sequoia axis.

## Conclusion

What these cases reveal is not a simple law of "YC punishes rebels," but several distinct boundaries: confidentiality, public criticism, gender issues, regulatory compliance, and harassment. YC treats them differently, and calling every removed founder a hero or martyr obscures those differences.

But one contradiction is real: an organization whose mission is to select independent thinkers must still enforce boundaries on independent thinkers. It's like parents teaching their children to think independently, then resenting them for questioning their decisions.

## Epilogue: Silicon Valley Radioactivity: Naval Ravikant

Naval Ravikant was never a YC alumnus, but his experience reveals how Silicon Valley's elite treats troublemakers.

Around 2005, Naval sued Benchmark Capital and August Capital, the VCs behind his first company Epinions, earning the label "radioactive mud" in Silicon Valley venture circles. He alleged the VCs had deceptively induced founders and employees to give up equity during Epinions' merger with Dealtime. The lawsuit left him nearly untouchable; those who dare expose the emperor's new clothes, however talented, get systematically isolated.

Naval did not disappear. He converted that marginalized experience into deep understanding of VC power structures, and in 2010 founded AngelList, a platform designed to break VC gatekeeping and let ordinary people invest in startups.

Today, Naval Ravikant is one of Silicon Valley's most influential and beloved investors and thinkers. His Twitter has millions of followers; his thoughts on wealth, happiness, and philosophy are treated as scripture. The VCs who once deemed him "radioactive" now compete to work with him — quite a turnaround, from radioactive mud to hot property.

To fully understand innovation, don't just watch the celebrated alumni on the Demo Day stage; pay attention to the exiles locked out of the servers too. It is they who, at the price of expulsion, map the system's boundaries.

Marginalization by the elite is not the end; it may be a new beginning. True rebels are not defeated by systems — they create new ones. Sometimes getting kicked out is the best possible start.
