Coffee is soy milk. Milk is soy milk too.

> Author: [Koutian Wu](https://www.linkedin.com/in/ktwu01/); [GitHub: ktwu01](https://github.com/ktwu01/)

One morning I held a coffee in one hand and a croissant in the other, took a bite, and scattered flakes all over the table. My first thought: this is just soy milk and youtiao.

Youtiao is a long fried dough stick, the classic Chinese breakfast partner for hot soy milk. Swap the cup, swap the bread, and the motion is identical. A hot drink, a crisp piece of carbohydrate. Dip, bite, sip.

Follow that thought and Beijing alone has two more.

Douzhi, a sour fermented bean drink, is soy milk. Jiaoquan, a thin crispy fried dough ring, is youtiao.

Luzhu, a hot offal stew, is soy milk. Huoshao, the flatbread soaked in it, is youtiao.

If one city can grow two, the rest of the world gets stranger still. The French dunk croissants into bowls of coffee. Italians have a cornetto with their cappuccino. Spaniards dip churros into hot chocolate. The British dunk biscuits in tea, and in India rusk, a hard twice-baked toast, goes into chai. Turkey pairs tea with simit, a sesame bread ring. Hong Kong has milk tea with a pineapple bun, Singapore has kopi with kaya toast, and Mongolia and Xinjiang have milk tea with fried dough, toasted millet, or naan.

So coffee is soy milk. So are milk, black tea, milk tea, and hot chocolate. A hot liquid plus a crisp carbohydrate seems to be standard issue for every culture.

It makes sense. After a night of sleep you are short on water and fuel: the hot drink rehydrates and warms you, the carbohydrate fills you up. Fried or dry-baked dough holds little water and keeps, and beans, tea, and coffee store well too. For ordinary people, cheap and long-lasting beat everything else. Stale bread comes back to life when you soak it, which is the whole idea behind yangrou paomo and the huoshao in luzhu. And the dipped end goes soft while the rest stays crisp, so every bite has both. Len Fisher, a physicist at the University of Bristol, even calculated how long to dunk a biscuit before it breaks, and won an Ig Nobel Prize for it.

A lot of what we think belongs only to our own culture turns out to be a similar answer people reached on similar days. Mornings are cold, people are hungry, money and time are short. Put that anywhere, and someone will pick up a hot cup and something to dip in it.

Next time I eat breakfast abroad and see someone dunk something into their cup, I will probably laugh: oh, you have soy milk and youtiao too.
