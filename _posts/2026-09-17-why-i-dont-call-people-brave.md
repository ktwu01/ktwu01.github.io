I don't call a person brave lightly. The word is heavy, and close to praise. To say it is to affirm how someone faced fear, cost, and responsibility.

> Author: [Koutian Wu](https://www.linkedin.com/in/ktwu01/); [GitHub: ktwu01](https://github.com/ktwu01/)

## Courage starts with seeing the cost

If a person walks into danger without seeing it, the most we know is that he dared. Courage also needs clarity: he knows what he might lose, he knows he could step back, and he still chooses to go forward.

Fear, then, does not weaken courage. Fear makes courage possible. A person with no fear has no need to summon courage. The hard part is to be afraid and act anyway. Here a person shows his limits, and also shows that within those limits there is still a choice.

Courage is not proven by success or failure either. Success may come from luck, and failure does not erase the risk a person took at the time. Outcomes still deserve judgment, and the judgment behind a choice can be wrong, but those questions should be kept apart from courage. A brave decision can still be a wrong one.

The word also carries a hint of moral approval. When someone takes a huge risk to hurt others, we call him bold, ruthless, or mad, and rarely brave. Risk and fear only tell us whether a person dared. They cannot tell us what he dared for. What the courage protects, and who finally bears the cost, matter just as much.

## Why "knowing shame" is close to courage

The *Doctrine of the Mean* in the *Book of Rites* says: "Love of learning is close to wisdom, earnest practice is close to benevolence, knowing shame is close to courage." The two words that matter most are "close to." Knowing shame can push a person toward courage, but it is not the same as courage.

"Shame" here can be read as a moral awareness: I see a gap between what I did and the standard I hold. Humiliation imposed from outside usually brings embarrassment first. Knowing shame asks me to admit that I really did something I cannot endorse. Once I admit it, I have a reason to change.

Knowing my limits answers a different question: what can I do, what can't I do, and how much risk will I carry? It makes a person careful, and it can also make him step back. Knowing shame asks instead: even with limited ability, is there something I still ought to do? If I back down now, will I betray a standard I believe in?

So knowing your limits keeps courage clear-headed, and knowing shame gives courage a direction. Without the first, courage easily turns into self-congratulation, or even pushes the cost onto others. Without the second, a person may understand his boundaries well and never find a reason to cross them.

Shame itself needs sorting. Shame about a specific act leaves room to correct it. Shame about one's own existence usually leaves only the urge to punish oneself. The first kind can awaken courage. The second is more likely to destroy it. Perhaps the *Doctrine of the Mean* says "close to" because knowing shame is only a starting point. A more mature courage has to know what it is willing to protect.

## Looking back at a brave decision

I have been looking back at a decision I made and asking: why do I think it was brave?

Being hard is not reason enough. Sometimes a hard thing is just a hard thing. Being misunderstood proves nothing either, since stubbornness also meets opposition. It is easy to give my past self the label "brave." The hard part is to put the fear, the judgment, and the responsibility of that moment back in front of me.

What cost did I actually see? What was I trying to protect? Did I make anyone else bear the consequences of my choice? If no one knew and no one praised it, would I still make the same decision?

These questions do not hand back a flattering answer on their own. They may even make me admit that the decision held courage alongside impulse, ignorance, or a refusal to give in. Human motives are rarely pure. Telling them apart does not diminish my past self. It only lets me understand that choice more honestly.

I now understand courage this way: a limited person, after seeing clearly his fear, his limits, and the price he may pay, still chooses to take responsibility for something he cannot lightly abandon, and is willing to bear the consequences that belong to him.

This also explains why I don't call a person brave lightly. To praise someone as brave is to admit that he had every reason to step back, yet still took a step forward and caught the weight that step brought.

Courage deserves its songs precisely because it belongs to ordinary people who fear, who err, and who get hurt. A limited person sees his limits and is still willing to carry the weight for what he cherishes. That is the most moving thing about human courage.
