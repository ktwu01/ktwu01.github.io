While you're still grinding your heart out on "if you just work hard enough, you can move up," you may not realize the system was never designed to cultivate you in the first place, it was designed to screen you.

> Author: [Koutian Wu](https://www.linkedin.com/in/ktwu01/); [GitHub: ktwu01](https://github.com/ktwu01/)

In observing different education systems and how societies operate, one phenomenon keeps recurring: many systems are not fundamentally about "cultivation" but use "screening" as their means. They create an atmosphere of "hard work enables upward mobility" to keep people motivated and orderly, yet the channels that truly let anyone break through the ceiling are extremely narrow. Such mechanisms have their historical and practical justification, but for the individuals inside them, seeing their nature clearly matters more than blindly conforming to them.

This article tries to explore: when we become aware of this structural contradiction, how should we live within it? How do we avoid being consumed by the system without turning into passive bystanders?

---

## I. The nature of the problem: screening logic and individual development

Many large systems in modern society, whether education, the workplace, or broader mechanisms of class mobility, tend to adopt a "funnel" design. The top must maintain scarcity to preserve its value; the bottom needs mobility to sustain the system's vitality. The most delicate part is the middle layer: enough people must be made to see hope and be willing to invest time and energy, yet not too many must be allowed to actually break through. This is a delicate balance and a systemic tension.

The direct consequence of this tension is that individuals easily fall into a kind of "arranged striving": competing, comparing, and exhausting themselves along a fixed track, never stopping to ask whether this track suits them, or whether this striving points toward goals they genuinely endorse.

The deeper problem is that when a person spends a long time in a state of being screened, their mode of thinking gradually internalizes that logic: defining self-worth by external standards, measuring accomplishment by rankings and recognition. This is a hidden form of self-alienation.

---

## II. How to respond: three basic strategies

Facing this structural environment, I think there are three core strategies.

### 1. Hone your own capabilities

No matter how the external environment changes, real capability is always the most reliable card in your hand. By capability, I mean not only professional skills, but also the ability to learn, to judge, to execute, and to make decisions under uncertainty.

In a system that favors screening over cultivation, the individual must become their own educator. This means actively seeking real learning resources and building a solid knowledge base, rather than merely chasing labels on a résumé. The accumulation of capability is compound-interest-like: slow at first, but once it crosses a certain critical point, it yields exponential returns.

Importantly, this honing should be oriented toward the real world. Capability that solves real problems and creates real value is what carries across systems.

### 2. Maintain independence

Independence first shows in thinking: don't do something unconditionally just because the outside world asks you to. Cultivate the habit of questioning: what logic sits behind this demand? Does it align with the values I endorse? Will it actually bring the long-term outcome I want?

The deeper form of independence is shifting the motive for action from "passively responding to external demands" to "actively thinking about what value I can bring to the world." The former is defensive and draining; the latter is creative and sustainable. When a person plans their actions with "what unique value can I offer" as the coordinate, they move from being the object being screened to a participant who actively defines the rules of the game.

This independence isn't rebellion; it's a clear-eyed autonomous choice. It means you can sometimes choose to cooperate with the system, but that cooperation is based on rational judgment, not blind obedience.

### 3. Have more options

The richness of your options is essentially risk management. It has two dimensions:

**First, build transferable options and fallbacks.** Don't bet all your chips on a single track or a single identity. Whether it's cross-domain skill reserves, the ability to live across geographies, or cross-industry networks, all of it creates an answer to "if this path fails, where else can I go." Diversity of options provides not only security but also the courage to say no.

**Second, do as much as possible of what you actually want to do, instead of being forced into homogenized competition.** "Involution" is essentially the situation where, when everyone uses the same standards and the same methods to compete for the same scarce resources, the marginal returns on everyone's input approach zero. The way to break involution isn't to compete harder, but to redefine the dimension of competition: find the field you're genuinely good at and love, and build an asymmetric advantage there.

Doing what you want to do doesn't mean escaping difficulty or pursuing comfort. On the contrary, it often requires greater courage and longer persistence. But the difference is that this persistence is self-chosen, and therefore has an intrinsic source of motivation.

---

## III. Conclusion: finding autonomous space within structural tension

We must admit that individuals can hardly change vast social systems in the short term. But it is equally true that the system's control over the individual is often overestimated. At every concrete point in a person's life, there is more room for choice than imagined, provided he has the ability to see the situation clearly and the courage to make an independent judgment.

Honing capability is to be able to seize opportunity when it comes; maintaining independence is to keep your inner coordinate from being drowned out by external noise; having options is to still have a fallback, confidence, and the capital for a fresh start should any single system fail.

These three reinforce one another and together compose a survival strategy for maintaining autonomy within structural tension. It isn't passive escape; it is active, constructive self-responsibility. The ultimate goal is not to defeat the system, but to live, within the system, as a person with genuine agency.
