Starting a petition, founding a club, and joining a research effort someone else organized look like three different things. They all run into the same question: who decides, who puts in the time, and when something goes wrong, who bears the consequences?

> Author: [Koutian Wu](https://www.linkedin.com/in/ktwu01/); [GitHub: ktwu01](https://github.com/ktwu01/)

Things like this are easy to frame as loyalty to friends. An acquaintance starts a petition, so you sign. A friend wants to found a club, so you lend your name. Someone organizes a research activity, so you pitch in. In public action and research collaboration, loyalty is the wrong frame. The right one is skin in the game.

Skin in the game looks at what people actually put in and what they stand to lose. Time, reputation, resources, and responsibility all count as investment. However much authority and benefit a person wants, they should be willing to bear a matching cost. The investments need not be identical, but authority, responsibility, and reward should line up as closely as possible.

Picture this: one person gathers a group, says "let's get started," then does nothing himself and only tells everyone else to work. He takes the organizer's power and leaves the entire cost with the participants. A relationship like that runs on one-sided investment from others.

Where people collaborate repeatedly and can see each other's effort, a safe strategy is conditional cooperation: I put in as much as the other person actually does. If they start investing, I keep cooperating. If they only give orders, I do not raise my stake on their behalf. This protects the people willing to cooperate, and it leaves the other side a way back into the partnership.

From an evolutionary point of view, unconditional cooperators are easy prey for free riders. If free riding costs nothing, it spreads, and eventually cooperation itself cannot survive. Adjusting your investment to what the other side actually does reduces exploitation and makes it easier for those willing to share the burden to stay.

The best strategy in game theory changes with the rules. What matters here is watching actions, and no single slogan covers every case. Before asking everyone to invest, has this person invested first? Of the risk he asks others to take, how much does he carry himself?

Before joining a petition, you should know whether its claims are accurate, whether the organizer is willing to take public responsibility, and what risk the signers will bear. A petition should be more than forwarding a paragraph that sounds right. Once your name is on it, you are vouching for the claim.

Founding a club works the same way. At the start everyone is happy to talk about the vision. The real work comes later: who writes the charter, who runs the events, who handles the money, who settles conflicts, who closes things down at the end. Names without responsibility soon turn a club into a few people carrying everyone else.

Joining a research effort someone else organized calls for asking these questions even more clearly. Who sets the research goal? How much time does each person put in? Who owns the data, the code, and the results? On what principle is authorship decided? If the project fails or stops halfway, how is the work already done counted? Leave these questions unasked, and loyalty only postpones the disagreement.

Loyalty can get someone to say yes once. It cannot keep a collaboration standing for long. Reliable collaboration does not ask everyone to invest equally. It asks everyone to know what they agreed to and to be willing to bear their share of the consequences.

Talking about skin in the game does not turn every relationship into a transaction. It is because we value relationships that we should not let loyalty paper over unclear authority and responsibility. The people who can share the consequences with you are the ones worth working with.
