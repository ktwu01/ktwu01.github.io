A business jet fell out of the sky over Florida yesterday, and all nine people on board died.

> Author: [Koutian Wu](https://www.linkedin.com/in/ktwu01/); [GitHub: ktwu01](https://github.com/ktwu01/)

The jet had taken off from Austin, the city I live in, on its way to the Bahamas. It was a Learjet 60 built in 2004. The crew reported icing and rough air, then an overspeed, and the flight data shows the plane dropping faster than 30,000 feet a minute near the end. It came down in a neighborhood in Palm Beach County, and nobody on the ground was hurt. The National Transportation Safety Board, the NTSB, is investigating, and it says the jet may have started breaking up before it hit the ground.

I asked Grok about it on X. Two questions. Is it easier to die on a private jet or on an airliner. And the one that stuck with me. Rich people can afford the safest seat in the sky, so why do they keep choosing the riskier one.

The yardstick everyone uses is fatal accidents per 100,000 flight hours. You count the fatal crashes, divide by the total hours flown, then scale it up. Airlines come out at about 0.006, which works out to roughly one fatal accident in 16 million hours of flying. Business aviation comes out between 0.1 and 0.3, so somewhere between one in a million hours and one in 330,000 hours. Put the two side by side and the private side is somewhere between 15 and 50 times deadlier than the airline side. Small planes flown by their owners are worse still, up around 0.65 to 0.87.

Fifty times sounds terrifying until you look at the base rate. One fatal accident in a million hours is still a tiny number. Fly private every week for a lifetime and the odds stay heavily on your side. Both things are true at once, the multiple is huge and the absolute risk is small, and people just pick the version that feels better.

The gap itself is not hard to explain. An airline is a machine for removing discretion. Two pilots, strict rest rules, dispatchers who can cancel a flight no matter how loudly the passengers complain, maintenance on a fixed clock, the same routes flown again and again. A private operation can copy almost all of that, and the best charter companies nearly match airline safety. Or it can be an older jet, a crew hired for the weekend, and a schedule set by the person in the back who really wants to be in the Bahamas tonight. The statistics average the careful and the casual together.

Grok answered my second question the way most people would. The rich are buying time. No security lines, no layovers, small airports close to where they actually want to be, wheels up whenever they say so. Time is the one thing money cannot manufacture more of, so they spend money, and a little risk, to buy some back.

I wrote back that this is not like skin in the game. Skin in the game, the way Nassim Taleb means it, is when the person making the decision eats the downside of that decision. The passenger in the back of a Learjet does eat the downside, all of it, in the worst possible way. So on paper Grok had a point, and I still felt something was off.

Here is the part I keep coming back to. When you can afford the safest option on the menu and still pick the riskier one, the risk stops looking like conviction. It looks like a price. You are not betting on anything. You are paying in a currency that is worth almost nothing to you, for a faster door to door trip. The bill just happens to be settled on the one day everything goes wrong at 35,000 feet.

The nine people on that plane were buying the same product, a Sunday flight straight to the Bahamas, no layover, no lines. Nothing about the purchase was irrational. That is the part I cannot stop thinking about.

A risk can be small, priced correctly, and still collect.
